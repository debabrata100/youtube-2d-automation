# E1 "When Will a Day Be 25 Hours Long?": script draft 1

Long episode, 16:9, target **10–12 minutes**. Narrator: **Gahanarka R1**. Arka: **Arka E3** (warm, curious, grown-up; chosen 22 Nov 2026).

**Title (Debabrata's idea, content plan E1):** **When Will a Day Be 25 Hours Long?** (32 characters). A "When…" shape after "Could…" (M1).

**Pays off M1's ending:** Arka asked "when do we get a twenty-five-hour day? Because I have plans for that hour." This episode answers it in the first minute, then shows who is building the hour, who keeps stealing it back, and what it would feel like.

**Angle:** "a 25-hour day in 200 million years" is a familiar headline, so the number is not the payoff; it's the cold open. The story is the **tug of war over the 25th hour**:
1. **The builder:** the Moon (callback to M1, kept short), with ice-age rebound quietly working against it.
2. **The bookkeeping:** leap seconds, the 27 seconds the world has added since 1972, and the 2035 decision to stop.
3. **The surprise:** since 2020 Earth has been speeding up (record short day, 5 July 2024), and the first *negative* leap second may be needed.
4. **The twist:** melting ice is now lengthening the day so fast it could overtake the Moon this century, and it has already pushed back that negative leap second by three years.
5. **The human angle:** our body clocks run about 24 h 11 min, and NASA's rover teams have already lived on a longer day.

**Structure device (repetition rule 13):** **the 25th-hour bar**: a thin bar under a clock face that fills from 0 to 60 minutes. Each chapter shows who adds to it and who drains it. M1 ran a backwards counter; Ep 5 a thermometer; Ep 4 a forward timeline.

### Chapter 1: The builder

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| d7 | Narrator | The main builder, you already know. The Moon pulls the oceans into bulges, Earth's spin drags them ahead, and the Moon pulls back on them like a brake. |
| d8 | Narrator | That brake adds about two point four milliseconds to the day every century. Far less than the blink of an eye. |
| d9 | Narrator | But something has been working against it for twenty thousand years. During the last ice age, ice sheets kilometers thick pressed down on Canada and Scandinavia. When the ice melted, the land started rising back up. It's still rising today. |
| d10 | Narrator | As the land rebounds, Earth gets a little rounder and a little more compact. And a spinning thing that pulls itself in spins faster, like a skater pulling in her arms. That takes back about point eight milliseconds a century. |
| d11 | Arka | So the Moon builds my hour, and Canada slowly un-builds it. |
| d12 | Narrator | Pretty much. Put them together, and for the last three thousand years the day has grown by about one point seven milliseconds a century. |
| d13 | Narrator | An hour is three point six million milliseconds. At that pace, filling it takes about two million centuries. Two hundred million years. |
| d14 | Narrator | How do we even measure milliseconds on a whole planet? Every day, radio telescopes on different continents point at the same quasars, blazing galaxies billions of light-years away, and time exactly when each signal arrives at each dish. |
| d15 | Narrator | Those arrival times shift as Earth turns. From them, scientists can measure the length of the day to a few millionths of a second. |
| d16 | Narrator | And in that time, everything else changes too. A year would have about three hundred and fifty days instead of three hundred and sixty-five. |
| d17 | Arka | Fifteen fewer days a year. And somehow, the year will still go too fast. |
| d18 | Narrator | But that's the slow story. Over a single lifetime, the day doesn't grow smoothly at all. And we've been keeping score. |

### Chapter 2: The second we keep adding

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| d19 | Narrator | Since 1972, the world's official time has come from atomic clocks, which tick at exactly the same rate forever. Earth doesn't. Its day has been running a millisecond or two longer than the atomic one. |
| d20 | Narrator | A millisecond a day doesn't sound like much. But after a year or two, it adds up to a whole second. So every time Earth falls almost a second behind, timekeepers add one extra second to the world's clocks. A leap second. |
| d21 | Narrator | It happens at the end of a day, usually the 31st of December or the 30th of June. The clock ticks past fifty-nine seconds to a sixtieth second, twenty-three fifty-nine and sixty, and only then reaches midnight. |
| d22 | Arka | So the planet runs late, and the entire world politely waits for it. |
| d23 | Narrator | Twenty-seven times since 1972. The last one was on the 31st of December, 2016. |
| d24 | Narrator | And computers hate it. A minute with sixty-one seconds has crashed servers and confused flight-booking systems. So in 2022, the world's timekeepers voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035, and let the clocks drift away from the Sun, slowly, by up to about a minute a century. |
| d25 | Narrator | Some companies have quietly found their own fix. Google, for example, doesn't add the extra second at midnight. It smears it out, running its clocks a tiny bit slow for the whole day around it. |
| d26 | Arka | That's exactly how I handle deadlines. |
| d27 | Narrator | But after that vote, something odd happened. The leap seconds had already stopped coming. Because Earth had started speeding up. |

### Chapter 3: The thief

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| d28 | Narrator | Since 2020, Earth has been spinning slightly faster than the atomic clocks. Days have been coming up short. |
| d29 | Narrator | Short days on their own aren't new. Even the weather changes the length of the day. Over a year, it shifts by about a millisecond, as the great winds of the atmosphere speed up and slow down, and the solid planet turns slightly slower or faster to balance them. |
| d30 | Narrator | But this was different. Year after year, the short days kept coming. |
| d31 | Narrator | On the 5th of July, 2024, Earth finished its turn one point six six milliseconds early. The shortest day since atomic clocks began measuring. And in the summer of 2025, several days came close to beating it. |
| d32 | Arka | The planet had a coffee. |
| d33 | Narrator | The main suspect is deep underground. Earth's liquid outer core swirls, and it trades spin with the solid planet around it. For about fifty years, the core has been slowing down, and the rest of Earth has been speeding up to make up for it. Why the core changed, nobody knows yet. |
| d34 | Narrator | If this keeps up, the clocks will need something that has never been done: a negative leap second. One second removed. The clock would jump from fifty-eight seconds to midnight, skipping a second entirely. |
| d35 | Narrator | And that's a problem. As geophysicist Duncan Agnew points out, plenty of software can add a second, but "few if any allow for removing a second." Nobody has ever tried it on the real world's clocks. |
| d36 | Arka | So we built a global system that can only ever run late. That feels very human. |
| d37 | Narrator | In 2024, Agnew worked out when that missing second would be needed. His answer surprised everyone, because something was already slowing Earth back down. Us. |

### Chapter 4: We joined in

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| d38 | Narrator | Since about 1990, the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica have been melting faster. The water flows off the poles and spreads through the oceans, much of it towards the equator, the widest part of the spinning planet. |
| d39 | Narrator | That's the skater again, but in reverse. Arms out. Earth slows down. |

| d43 | Arka | We finally beat the Moon at something, and it's this. |
| d44 | Narrator | To be clear, this won't bring your extra hour any sooner in a way you'd notice. It's milliseconds. But it is the clearest sign yet that we've become big enough to change how fast the planet turns. |

### Chapter 5: Living on a longer day

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| d46 | Narrator | Your body has its own clock. In 1999, Charles Czeisler's team at Harvard kept volunteers in carefully dim, timeless rooms and measured it. On average, it runs about twenty-four hours and eleven minutes. Close to a day, but not exactly. |
| d47 | Narrator | Every morning, sunlight quietly resets it. A 25-hour day would ask it to stretch another forty-nine minutes. |
| d48 | Narrator | Some people have already tried living on a longer day. A day on Mars lasts twenty-four hours and thirty-nine minutes. When NASA lands a rover, the team on Earth often lives on Mars time for the first few months, starting work about forty minutes later every single day. |
| d49 | Narrator | For the Spirit and Opportunity rovers in 2004, team members wore special watches that ran on Mars time. Some wore two: one for Earth, one for Mars. After a couple of weeks, their working day had moved from morning to the middle of the night. |
| d50 | Arka | Forty minutes later every day. That's not a lie-in, that's a very slow jet lag. |
| d51 | Narrator | That's how team members described it. And for the Curiosity rover in 2012, the team switched back to Earth time after three months, as planned. |
| d52 | Narrator | But the 25-hour day won't arrive in a few months. It will creep in over two hundred million years, longer than the dinosaurs ruled the Earth. Life will have had all that time to adjust its clocks, one millisecond at a time. |

## Honesty notes

- **"200 million years" assumes today's average rate continues.** It won't exactly: ocean shapes change as continents move, and M1 showed the day once stalled for a billion years. Narration says "about" and "roughly"; the description should say it's an estimate at today's average rate.
- **The core explanation is a leading hypothesis, not settled.** d33 says "the main suspect" and "nobody knows yet" why it changed.
- **Climate numbers are milliseconds.** d44 says plainly that you'd never notice it; the point is scale, not alarm. Calm, non-political tone (compliance rule 10).
- **No earthquake example:** the 2011 Japan earthquake shortened the day by 1.8 µs (NASA JPL, Richard Gross), but it's a real-world tragedy; left out on purpose.
- **Open loop:** d55–d56 tease **E7 (Apophis)**. Its script must be at least drafted before E1 is published, or d55–d57 change.

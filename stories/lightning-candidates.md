## Candidate A text (selected)

1. A lightning bolt can be five times hotter than the Sun's surface.
2. It begins inside a storm cloud, where strong winds toss ice upward.
3. Ice crystals and soft hail collide and trade electric charge.
4. Positive charge rises to the top. Negative charge sinks to the bottom.
5. Then a faint channel of charge zigzags down, step by step.
6. When it meets a spark rising from the ground, the air flashes white-hot.
7. That blast of heat makes the air explode outward. We hear thunder.

## Candidate B text (reserve)

Why does a storm cloud fire bolts of electricity? Rising winds carry ice high into the cloud. Crystals and soft hail collide, swapping charge. The top turns positive, the bottom negative. A faint path of charge creeps down in steps. A spark leaps up from the ground to meet it. The connection flashes, heating the air so fast it booms as thunder.

## Candidate C text (reserve)

Imagine air hotter than the Sun, ripped open in a millionth of a second. That's lightning. Inside a thunderstorm, winds hurl ice and soft hail together, and each collision moves electric charge. Positive charge drifts to the top, negative charge to the base. A faint channel zigzags toward the ground. A spark rises to meet it, the circuit closes, and the air flashes white-hot, exploding outward as thunder.

## Fact check (sources consulted before writing)

- ~50,000 °F (about 28,000–30,000 °C), "five times hotter than the surface of the Sun": NOAA National Weather Service (weather.gov/safety/lightning-temperature) and NOAA SciJinks. A uses "can be" because the figure refers to the heated channel at peak.
- Charge separation: ice crystals and graupel ("soft hail") collide in updrafts; lighter, positively charged crystals are carried up, and heavier, negatively charged graupel stays lower (NOAA JetStream; NSSL primer). The narration simplifies the usual small lower positive pocket away.
- A faint negative **stepped leader** zigzags down in roughly 50-yard steps; positive **streamers** rise from the ground; their connection drives the visible **return stroke** (NSSL primer; NWS training material).
- Thunder is the sound of air expanding explosively after being heated in microseconds (NSSL primer).
- Simplifications to keep in mind: most lightning never reaches the ground (intracloud flashes are more common), and the exact charging mechanism is still debated. The video shows the common negative cloud-to-ground case.

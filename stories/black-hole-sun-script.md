# S2 "Would a Black-Hole Sun Suck Us In?": script draft 1

Long episode, 16:9, target **10–12 minutes**. Narrator: **Gahanarka R1**. Arka: **Arka E3** (chosen 22 Nov 2026).

**Pays off E7's ending:** after watching Earth bend an asteroid's path, Arka asks what the strongest pull there is would do: "If the Sun became a black hole, would it pull us in?"

**Title options (≤ 50, honest what-if, myth-bust shape like E7):**
1. **Would a Black-Hole Sun Suck Us In? (No, But…)** (45) ← **chosen (Debabrata, 22 Nov)**
2. **If the Sun Became a Black Hole, Would We Fall In?** (49)
3. **Black Holes Don't Suck. Here's the Proof.** (40)

**Thumbnail ideas:** (1) Earth calmly on its orbit around a tiny black dot where the Sun used to be, text **"WE'D STAY. IN THE DARK."**; (2) a vacuum cleaner crossed out next to a black hole, **"NOT A VACUUM"**; (3) Arka holding a tiny black marble, **"THIS IS THE SUN"**. Arka on every thumbnail, same corner mark (brand rule, 22 Nov).

**Angle (competitor check, 22 Nov):** black-hole what-ifs are owned by giants (What If "Earth Sucked Into a Black Hole?" 9M; Kurzgesagt 15–30M; Stargaze "Black Hole Entered Our Solar System" 4.8M), mostly 1–5 years old. The exact question "would a black-hole Sun suck us in" has only one small recent video (12K). Our edge is the honest myth-bust: the famous answer ("we'd fall in") is wrong, and the real answer is stranger.

**Not a repeat of Ep 5:** Ep 5 made the Sun *vanish* (light *and* gravity gone: Earth flew off and froze). Here the light goes but **the grip stays**: Earth keeps its exact orbit around something it can't see. Ep 5's freezing gets one line and a callback, no repeat.

**Structure device (repetition rule 13):** **two meters side by side**, GRIP and LIGHT. GRIP stays at 100% for the whole episode (until we fly in, chapter 3); LIGHT drops to zero 8 min 20 s after the swap. Ep 4 timeline, Ep 5 thermometer, M1 rewind counter, E1 25th-hour bar, E7 ride clock.

**Middle-stretch rule:** each chapter opens differently: a magic trick (cold open), a clock (ch 1), a look around (ch 2), a dare (ch 3), a real star (ch 4), Arka's worry (ch 5).

### Cold open: the swap

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b1 | Narrator | Imagine a magic trick. At noon, someone takes the Sun and squeezes it. All of it, every gram, crushed into a ball about six kilometres across. |
| b2 | Narrator | That's a black hole with exactly the Sun's mass. Most people know what happens next. Earth gets sucked in. |
| b3 | Arka | I mean… that's what black holes do, isn't it? |
| b4 | Narrator | It's the most famous answer in space. And it's wrong. Earth would stay right where it is. |
| b5 | Narrator | So what *would* happen? What would you see in the sky? And how close would you have to get before a black hole really does pull you in? |

### Chapter 1: Noon, plus eight minutes

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b6 | Narrator | Twelve o'clock. The Sun has just been crushed. And down on Earth, nothing happens. |
| b7 | Narrator | The sunlight already on its way keeps arriving for eight minutes and twenty seconds. If you watched our Sun-disappearing episode, you know that delay. |
| b8 | Narrator | But here's what's different. Last time, when the Sun vanished, its gravity vanished too, and Earth flew off in a straight line. This time, the mass is still there. Every gram of it. |
| b9 | Narrator | So look at the grip meter. One hundred percent. It doesn't even flicker. |
| b10 | Arka | But it's a black hole now. Isn't its pull stronger? |
| b11 | Narrator | Only up close. The pull of anything, a star, a planet, a black hole, depends on just two things: how much mass it has, and how far away you are. Same mass, same distance, same pull. |
| b12 | Narrator | So Earth keeps its orbit. Same path, same speed, same three hundred and sixty-five days. Mars, Jupiter, all of them, carry on as if nothing changed. |
| b13 | Narrator | Then, at eight minutes and twenty seconds, the light meter drops to zero. |
| b14 | Arka | So we keep going around the Sun… without a Sun. |
| b15 | Narrator | Exactly. Earth would freeze, just as it did in our last thought experiment, but slowly, safely, on schedule, circling something it can no longer see. |
| b15b | Narrator | And the sky above it would look stranger than you think. |

### Chapter 2: Look around

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b16 | Narrator | Let's step outside on that first dark afternoon and look up. Where the Sun was, what do you see? |
| b17 | Arka | A giant black hole, swallowing the sky? |
| b18 | Narrator | Nothing. The black hole is about six kilometres across, a hundred and fifty million kilometres away. That's like trying to see a single grain of sand on the far side of a country. |
| b19 | Narrator | Its gravity still bends the light of stars passing behind it. Line one up exactly and the star would smear into a tiny ring. But the whole effect fits in a patch about a twentieth of the Moon's width. You'd need binoculars to notice. |
| b20 | Narrator | And everything else? The stars come out in the middle of the afternoon. The planets go dark, because they only ever shone with borrowed sunlight. But they're all still there, still circling. |
| b21 | Narrator | The Moon still orbits Earth. It just stops shining. And the tides keep coming, because the Moon does most of that work, and the Sun's share is still pulling too. |
| b22 | Arka | So the scariest object in the universe arrives, and the main thing we notice is… the lights went out. |

### Chapter 3: How close is too close?

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b23 | Narrator | Fine. Let's dare it. We take a spacecraft and fly straight towards the black hole. When does it start pulling us in? |
| b24 | Narrator | Not for a long, long time. Fly in until you're where the Sun's surface used to be, about seven hundred thousand kilometres from the centre, and the pull is exactly what it would be at the Sun's surface today. Strong, but nothing strange. |
| b25 | Narrator | Only when we go closer than that, into space the Sun used to fill, does anything new happen. Here, the pull keeps growing in a way no star allows, because there's no surface to stop us. |
| b26 | Arka | So that's where the vacuum cleaner idea comes from. |
| b27 | Narrator | Partly. NASA puts it plainly: a black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner. It only takes what comes very close. |
| b28 | Narrator | How close? About seventeen hundred kilometres out, the difference between the pull on your head and your feet starts stretching you. Scientists really do call it spaghettification. |
| b29 | Narrator | At about nine kilometres from the centre, there's no safe orbit left. And at about three, the event horizon: the point where not even light can climb back out. |
| b29b | Narrator | That three-kilometre figure was first worked out in a hospital bed. At the end of 1915, a German astronomer named Karl Schwarzschild was serving in the army near the Russian front. Sick, within earshot of the guns, he solved Einstein's brand-new equations for a single round star, and posted the answer to Einstein. |
| b29c | Narrator | Einstein wrote back that he hadn't expected anyone could find an exact solution so simply. Schwarzschild died a few months later, in May 1916. He never knew his maths described something real. |
| b29d | Arka | He worked out a black hole from a hospital bed, near a war. And never got to see one. |
| b30 | Arka | Three kilometres. So to get sucked in, Earth would have to come from a hundred and fifty million kilometres away to basically on top of it. |
| b31 | Narrator | And Earth isn't going anywhere. It's moving sideways at about thirty kilometres a second. That's what an orbit is: falling, and missing, forever. |
| b31b | Narrator | Still, that's maths on paper. Is there anywhere we can actually watch it happen? There is. |

### Chapter 4: Proof, from a real black hole

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b32 | Narrator | This isn't only theory. Twenty-six thousand light-years away, at the centre of our galaxy, there's a real black hole, about four million times the mass of the Sun. |
| b33 | Narrator | And stars orbit it. One of them, a star called S2, goes around it every sixteen years, swinging in to less than twenty billion kilometres. Two teams have tracked it since the early nineteen-nineties: one led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany, one by Andrea Ghez in California. Ghez's first proposal to watch these stars was turned down. |
| b34 | Arka | So a star just… circles a black hole. Like we circle the Sun. |
| b35 | Narrator | On a stretched, looping path, and fast. But yes. It orbits, misses, comes back, every sixteen years. And when it swung closest in 2018, it behaved exactly as Einstein's theory said it would. In 2020, Genzel and Ghez shared the Nobel Prize for proving the black hole is there. A hundred and five years after a sick soldier worked out what it would be like. |
| b36 | Narrator | If a star can orbit a black hole four million times heavier than the Sun, Earth can orbit one that weighs exactly the same. |

### Chapter 5: Will the Sun ever do it?

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| b37 | Arka | Okay, but I'm still a little worried. Could our Sun actually become a black hole one day? |
| b38 | Narrator | No. It isn't heavy enough. To end its life as a black hole, a star needs about twenty times the Sun's mass. |
| b39 | Narrator | Our Sun has a quieter future. In billions of years it will swell into a red giant, puff off its outer layers, and leave behind a white dwarf: a glowing ember about the size of Earth, slowly cooling for longer than the universe has existed so far. |
| b40 | Arka | So no black hole. Just a very long retirement. |
| b41 | Narrator | And no magic trick. The swap can't happen. But thinking it through tells us something real: gravity doesn't care what a thing looks like. Only how much there is, and how far away you are. |

## Fact check (sources consulted 22 Nov 2026)

| Claim | Line(s) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A Sun-mass black hole is about 6 km across (radius ≈ 3 km) | b1, b18, b29 | The Wire (Science): Schwarzschild radius of a Sun-mass black hole ≈ 2.96 km. Diameter ≈ 6 km. |
| Earth would not be sucked in; orbit unchanged; only temperature changes | b4, b8, b12, b42 | NASA Imagine the Universe: "the only thing that would change would be the Earth's temperature. The gravity… would not change"; Chandra: Earth would continue to orbit without being pulled in; NASA Science ("10 questions"): would not affect the orbits of the planets; ESO: planets would stay on their orbits. |
| Light keeps arriving for 8 min 20 s | b7, b13 | Ep 5 fact table (Live Science; IFLScience). |
| Gravity depends only on mass and distance | b11 | NASA JPL (Marc Rayman): the pull of the Sun, a black hole or anything else depends only on its mass and distance. |
| Ep 5 callback: Sun vanishing removes gravity, Earth flies off | b8, b15 | Ep 5 script (`stories/ghost-sun-script.md`). |
| "Like a grain of sand on the far side of a country" | b18 | **Calculated:** 6 km at 1.5 × 10⁸ km = 4 × 10⁻⁸ rad. A 0.1 mm grain at that angle sits ~2,500 km away (roughly India's width). Keep "a country". |
| Lensing: a tiny ring, patch ~1/20 of the Moon's width | b19 | **Calculated (re-checked 22 Nov):** Einstein radius θ_E = √(4GM/(c²D)) for a Sun-mass lens at D = 1 AU and a distant star = √(5.9 km / 1.496×10⁸ km) = 1.98×10⁻⁴ rad ≈ **41″** (ring diameter ~82″); full Moon ≈ 1,865″ → about 1/23. Draft 1 said ~0.04″: an arithmetic slip, a thousand times too small. Naked-eye resolution is ~60″, so the ring is at the edge of the eye's limit and only when a star lines up; "binoculars" is honest. |
| Planets go dark (reflected light); Moon stops shining | b20, b21 | Planets and the Moon shine by reflected sunlight (basic astronomy). |
| Tides continue: the Moon does most of it; the Sun's share remains (same mass) | b21 | NOAA (Ep 5 fact table): Sun's tidal force ≈ 46% of the Moon's; same mass → same tidal pull. |
| At the Sun's old surface (~700,000 km), the pull equals today's surface gravity | b24 | Follows from b11 (mass and distance only); Sun radius ≈ 696,000 km (standard). |
| "Not a cosmic vacuum cleaner" | b27 | NASA, quoted by IFLScience (2024): "Contrary to popular myth, a black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner." Under 15 words. |
| Spaghettification starts ~1,700 km out (for a person) | b28 | **Calculated:** tidal acceleration across a 2 m body Δa = 2GM·L/r³; set Δa ≈ 10 g → r = (2·1.327×10²⁰·2/98)^⅓ ≈ 1,760 km. The threshold is a choice; "about 1,700" with "starts stretching you". |
| No stable orbit inside ~9 km (ISCO = 3 Schwarzschild radii); event horizon ~3 km | b29 | **Calculated:** ISCO = 6GM/c² = 3 × 2.95 km ≈ 8.9 km (non-spinning black hole, standard GR). |
| Earth moves at about 30 km/s | b31 | Standard (Earth's orbital speed 29.8 km/s). |
| Sgr A*: 26,000 light-years; ~4 million solar masses | b32 | Max Planck Society (MPG): 26,000 light-years. Mass: Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics (MPE): 4.3 million solar masses (GRAVITY). Script "about four million" is a safe rounding; could say "four point three million". |
| S2 orbits every 16 years; closest < 20 billion km; tracked since 1992; 2018 closest pass matched Einstein | b33, b35 | ESO blog: 16-year orbit, observing since 1992, closest approach May 2018; MPG: within 20 billion km (120 AU), rosette orbit confirming Einstein; A&A 2018: gravitational redshift detected at the 2018 pericentre. |
| Sun can't become a black hole; needs ~20× its mass | b38 | NASA: "The Sun would need to be about 20 times more massive to end its life as a black hole." Space.com: around 20 times or more. |
| Red giant → white dwarf about Earth's size, cooling for trillions of years | b39 | NASA (red giant ~1 billion years, then white dwarf); Space.com/inkl: white dwarf roughly Earth's size; Astronomy.com: won't become a black dwarf for trillions of years; none exist yet because the universe isn't old enough. Script: "longer than the universe has existed so far". |

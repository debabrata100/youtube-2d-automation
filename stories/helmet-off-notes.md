# Episode 2 — "What happens if you take your helmet off in space?"

Approved 2026-11-16. Narrator + Arka (gold astronaut mascot, second voice). Main 16:9 (~80 s) and a hook-first Short (~28 s).

## Fact check (sources consulted 2026-11-16)

| Claim in script | Basis |
|---|---|
| About fifteen seconds of consciousness | Useful consciousness in vacuum is roughly 9–15 s (ESA/FAA guidance as reported by New Space Economy, 2026; Live Science quoting ESA's Stefaan de Mey: "10 to 15 seconds"). Jim LeBlanc stayed conscious about 14 s (1966). |
| Breathe out; holding your breath tears the lungs | Expanding air in the lungs causes barotrauma (NASA/Landis; Live Science; Science Notes). |
| You would not explode; you swell | Skin and circulation hold the body together; tissue swells from ebullism (Space.com, Paul Sutter; dog studies: swelling "like an inflated goatskin bag", Scientific American). |
| Blood does not boil; saliva on the tongue does | Blood stays pressurised inside vessels; exposed moist surfaces boil. LeBlanc's last memory was saliva boiling off his tongue (Guinness World Records entry, 14 Dec 1966). |
| You don't freeze quickly | Vacuum carries little heat away; heat loss is slow (NASA explanation, via New Space Economy and Science Notes). |
| Sunburn in sunlight | Unfiltered solar UV causes severe sunburn (Science Notes). |
| Lungs work in reverse, pulling oxygen out of blood | The very low outside pressure rapidly de-oxygenates blood through the lungs; deoxygenated blood reaches the brain after ~9–12 s. |
| Survivable if back to air within about a minute and a half | Animal studies: dogs exposed to near-vacuum up to 90 s survived; 2 min or more was often fatal (Brooks AFB 1965, via Scientific American; NASA Bioastronautics Data Book via Landis). |
| 1966 NASA vacuum-chamber accident; he was fine | Jim LeBlanc, Houston, 14 Dec 1966: suit lost pressure in a vacuum chamber, chamber repressurised to a safe level in ~87 s, no long-term effects (Guinness World Records). |

## Honesty notes

- The on-screen counter in the main episode steps with the narrated numbers; it is storytelling, not a stopwatch. In the Short it runs in real time between "fifteen seconds" and "zero".
- The 1966 engineer is drawn as a generic stick astronaut, not a likeness.

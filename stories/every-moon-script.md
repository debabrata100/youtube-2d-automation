# What Would Kill You First on Every Moon in the Solar System

Draft 4 · 2026-11-16 · N = narrator, A = Arka

**Structure:** the tension builds through the video. Two safe moons come first, then the weird ones, then the cold ones, then a radiation countdown as the climax, and Titan last as the twist. Every moon has a **specific way to kill you** and a **survival meter** card. Arka keeps picking a moon to live on and gets shot down 4 times, then the Titan payoff.

---

## Cold open (hook)
**N:** On one moon in our solar system, you could step off a cliff and fall for twelve whole minutes.
**N:** On another, walking too fast would launch you into space.
**N:** And on one of them, you could take off your spacesuit, strap on wings, and fly.
**N:** Now, without a suit, almost any moon kills you in about fifteen seconds. So you get a suit. A really good one, with eight hours of air.
**N:** The question is, what kills you first anyway? We'll save the deadliest moon for last. And the one where you can fly comes after that.
**A:** Ooh. I'm picking one to live on.
**N:** Of course you are.

## The safe ones

### Our Moon — survival meter: 8 hours, then the dust wins
**N:** We start at home. Our Moon is the only one people have walked on, so we know exactly what goes wrong.
**N:** The ground swings from over one hundred degrees in sunlight to below minus one hundred and seventy in shadow. Your suit handles both.
**N:** What it can't handle is the dust. Moon dust has never been smoothed by wind or water. It's sharp like broken glass, and it clings to everything.
**N:** Astronauts said it smelled like burnt gunpowder. After just three moonwalks, the Apollo seventeen suits were already wearing out.
**N:** So on the Moon, the dust slowly chews through your suit. Stay long enough, and something gives.

### Callisto — survival meter: 8 hours of air, and nothing else
**N:** Out at Jupiter, Callisto is the safest big moon in the outer solar system. The radiation here is even lower than on the International Space Station.
**A:** Callisto! Safe! I pick Callisto!
**N:** It's minus one hundred and forty degrees, dark, and covered in more craters than anywhere we know. Nothing happens here. Ever.
**A:** …Not this one.

## Act 1 · The weird ones

### Phobos and Deimos — survival meter: until you drift away
**N:** The two little moons of Mars. From Phobos, Mars fills a huge part of the sky.
**A:** Ooh, a view! This one!
**N:** But these moons are so small, their gravity barely holds you. On parts of Phobos, just walking faster than about five kilometers per hour could lift you off the ground for good.
**N:** You'd push off for a step, and the ground would slowly fall away beneath your boots.
**N:** On Deimos, one strong jump would do it. And then you'd drift, watching Mars turn, until your air ran out.
**A:** Not this one either.

### Miranda — survival meter: 12 minutes of falling
**N:** At Uranus, the moon Miranda has what may be the tallest cliff in the solar system, possibly twenty kilometers high. That's more than twice the height of Everest.
**N:** Step off the edge, and Miranda's gravity is so weak that you'd fall for about twelve minutes. Twelve silent minutes, watching the ground get closer.
**N:** You'd hit the bottom at about two hundred kilometers per hour. Without protection, that's the end.
**N:** But NASA scientists think you could survive it with the right airbags.
**A:** I brought one!

### Enceladus — survival meter: until you stand in the wrong place
**N:** At Saturn, little Enceladus is the brightest object in the solar system, covered in fresh snow.
**A:** It's snowing! This one, this one!
**N:** That snow comes from geysers. Near the south pole, long cracks called tiger stripes blast water and ice into space at over a thousand kilometers per hour.
**N:** Stand over a vent, and the jet could throw you high into the sky. In gravity this weak, you'd be up there a long time.
**N:** And if you miss your landing, the tiger stripes are cracks about half a kilometer deep.
**A:** Not this one either.

## Act 2 · The cold ones

### Triton and Charon — survival meter: as long as your battery lasts
**N:** Now we go far from the Sun. Neptune's moon Triton is minus two hundred and thirty-five degrees, one of the coldest places ever measured.
**N:** It orbits Neptune backwards, and it still has geysers, blasting dark nitrogen eight kilometers into the sky.
**N:** Further out, Pluto's moon Charon is almost as cold. From Charon, Pluto hangs in the exact same spot in the sky, forever. It never rises, and never sets.
**N:** Here, the cold doesn't kill you fast. Your suit is like a thermos. But every step on the frozen ground pulls heat from your boots, and your heaters drain the battery to fight it.
**N:** When the battery dies, the cold wins. Slowly. From your fingers and toes inward.
**A:** Hot chocolate. Lots of hot chocolate.

## Act 3 · The radiation countdown

**N:** Now the deadliest part of the solar system: Jupiter's radiation belt. You can't see it. You can't feel it. But your suit's radiation alarm can.
**N:** And the closer to Jupiter you go, the faster it ticks.

### Ganymede — survival meter: about 2 months
**N:** Ganymede is the biggest moon in the solar system, bigger than the planet Mercury. It's also the only moon with its own magnetic field, a weak shield against the radiation.
**N:** It's weak, but it helps. Here, a deadly dose would take about two months to build up.
**N:** So Ganymede is fine for a visit. Not for a home.

### Europa — survival meter: about 1 day
**N:** Closer in, Europa. Smooth white ice, hiding an ocean that may hold twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans.
**A:** An ocean! Aliens, maybe! This one!
**N:** Up on the surface, the alarm never stops. You'd take in a deadly dose in about a day.
**N:** Your only chance is to dig. A few meters of ice would shield you completely.
**A:** So I'd live… in an igloo.
**N:** On an alien ocean. Yes.
**A:** …Not this one either.

### Io — survival meter: about 3 hours
**N:** And the deadliest moon of all: Io, the most volcanic place in the solar system. It has about four hundred active volcanoes, some blasting sulfur four hundred kilometers into space.
**N:** But the volcanoes aren't what gets you. Io sits deep inside Jupiter's radiation belt.
**N:** On Io, your suit's alarm turns into one long scream. You'd absorb a deadly dose in about three hours, long before your air runs out.
**N:** And the worst part? You wouldn't feel a thing until it was far too late.
**A:** Get me out of here.

## The twist · Titan — survival meter: no pressure suit needed
**N:** And finally, the moon we promised. Saturn's moon Titan.
**N:** Titan has air. Thick air, about one and a half times the pressure on Earth. So you don't need a pressure suit at all.
**N:** You'd still need an oxygen mask, and the warmest coat ever made, because it's minus one hundred and seventy-nine degrees.
**N:** It even rains here. But the rain is liquid methane, filling lakes and seas bigger than some on Earth.
**N:** And with gravity this low and air this thick, you could strap wings to your arms and actually fly.
**A:** This one. It's this one. We're going to this one.
**A:** And if I crash, I brought an airbag.

## Closing
**N:** So, almost every moon in our solar system would get you in the end. Some would launch you, some would freeze you slowly, and some would poison you with radiation you can't even feel.
**N:** But one would let you fly.
**N:** Which moon would you pick? Tell us in the comments.
**A:** Titan. Obviously.

## Fact-check notes (sources gathered 2026-11-16)
- **Moon:**
  - About +120 °C in sunlight, about −170 °C or colder in shadow (NASA).
  - Astronauts compared the dust smell to spent gunpowder (Apollo crews; NASA).
  - Apollo 17 suit wear from dust after 3 EVAs is documented in NASA dust-effects reviews (Gaier, 2005).
- **Callisto:** about 0.1 mSv/day at the surface, below the ISS; the most heavily cratered known object; about −139 °C.
- **Phobos and Deimos:**
  - Phobos escape velocity about 11 m/s; a 2018 study found that faster than about 5 km/h can lift you off in some regions.
  - Deimos escape velocity about 5–6 m/s.
  - From Phobos, Mars spans about 40° of the sky.
- **Miranda:** NASA APOD (2016): cliff about 20 km (other estimates 5–15 km), about 12-minute fall, about 200 km/h, possibly survivable with airbags.
- **Enceladus:**
  - Jets about 1,300 km/h (Cassini, 2008); tiger stripes about 500 m deep.
  - About 200 kg/s total through more than 100 jets. My estimate is about 2 kg/s × 400 m/s ≈ 800 N per jet, enough to throw a suited person (weight about 17 N there) upward, but not to escape speed (240 m/s).
- **Triton:** −235 °C, retrograde orbit, nitrogen plumes about 8 km (Voyager 2).
- **Charon:** about −220 °C; Pluto and Charon are tidally locked to each other.
- **Radiation (unshielded surface, per day):** Ganymede about 0.08 Sv, Europa about 5.4 Sv, Io about 36 Sv. Survival times assume about 5 Sv is deadly (≈ 2 months, 1 day, 3 hours). A deadly dose kills days to weeks later; "you wouldn't feel a thing until it was too late" reflects that.
- **Ganymede:** 5,268 km wide vs Mercury's 4,879 km; it has a magnetic field (Galileo).
- **Europa:** an ocean with about 2× Earth's ocean water (NASA, commonly cited); a few metres of ice blocks most of the radiation.
- **Io:** about 400 active volcanoes; plumes up to about 400 km (Hubble, Pele).
- **Titan:** 1.5× Earth's pressure, −179 °C, methane rain and lakes (Kraken Mare is comparable to or larger than the Caspian Sea), human-powered flight possible.

**A:** Excuse me?
**N:** It's on Miranda, a tiny moon of Uranus. The cliff could be twenty kilometers high, more than twice as tall as Mount Everest.
**N:** But Miranda's gravity is so weak that the fall would take about twelve minutes.
**A:** Twelve minutes? What do I do for twelve minutes?
**N:** Enjoy the view. You'd hit the bottom at about two hundred kilometers per hour. Fast, but NASA scientists think you could survive it with the right airbags.
**A:** I brought one!

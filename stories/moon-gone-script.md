# M3.8 "What If the Moon Disappeared Tonight?": script draft 1

Long episode, 16:9, target 10–12 minutes. Narrator (Bill L. Oxley) plus Arka (Jessica). Arka floats in the empty spot where the Moon used to be.

**Angle:** most videos say "Earth tips over and we all die". Newer science says something calmer, and stranger. The title keeps the drama; the video gives the facts.

**Title options:**
1. What If the Moon Disappeared Tonight?
2. The Moon Vanishes Tonight. Here's What Really Happens

## Script

### Cold open: 9:00 PM

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m1 | Narrator | Tonight, at exactly nine o'clock, the Moon disappears. No explosion. No warning. It's simply gone. |
| m2 | Arka | Uh... where did it go? I was standing right here! |
| m3 | Narrator | For a moment, nothing changes. The Moon is so far away that its last light is still on the way. It takes about one and a third seconds to reach us. |
| m4 | Narrator | Then the sky goes out. |
| m5 | Narrator | You've probably heard what happens next. Earth tips over. The seasons go crazy. We all freeze. Everyone dies. |
| m6 | Narrator | That's what most videos say. It's not what the newest science says. So let's find out what would really happen: the first night, the first week, and the next million years. |
| m7 | Arka | And whether I need a new job. |

### Chapter 1: The first night

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m8 | Narrator | On a clear night, a full Moon lights the ground about a hundred times more than the stars alone. Without it, every night becomes the darkest kind of night. Forever. |
| m9 | Narrator | For us, that mostly means more streetlights. For a lot of animals, it's a much bigger deal. |
| m10 | Narrator | Even we might notice. In 2021, scientists tracked the sleep of Indigenous communities in Argentina and of college students in Seattle. In the few days before a full moon, people went to bed later and slept less, even in a city full of electric light. |
| m11 | Narrator | Without the Moon, that monthly rhythm in our sleep would have nothing left to follow. |
| m12 | Arka | So the Moon was keeping you up all along? Rude. |
| m13 | Narrator | Take lions. In the Serengeti, lions eat best on dark nights, when there's no moonlight to give them away. Around the full Moon, their bellies are emptier, and they have to hunt more by day. |
| m14 | Narrator | So without the Moon, every single night is a hunter's night. Great news for lions. Not such great news for everything they eat. |
| m15 | Arka | Note to self: never be a zebra. |
| m16 | Narrator | Now go north, to the Arctic winter, where the Sun doesn't rise for weeks. Tiny sea animals called zooplankton follow the Moon instead. Across the whole Arctic Ocean, they rise and sink with it, and at every full moon, they dive deep to hide from hunters. |
| m17 | Narrator | Take the Moon away, and their clock simply stops. |
| m18 | Arka | So who tells them when to go to bed? |
| m19 | Narrator | Nobody. That's the problem. |

### Chapter 2: The first week

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m20 | Narrator | The next morning, someone at the beach notices something odd. The tide isn't coming in the way it should. |
| m21 | Narrator | Tides happen because the Moon's gravity pulls on our oceans. The Sun pulls too, but it's so far away that its effect is much weaker. |
| m22 | Narrator | Without the Moon, the Sun is left on its own, and the tides shrink to about a third of their size. |
| m23 | Narrator | Beaches that flood and drain twice a day would barely move. Mudflats, tide pools and mangrove forests, whole worlds that live between high and low tide, would slowly start to change. |
| m24 | Narrator | Tides also do something you'd never notice from a beach. As they sweep over underwater mountains, they stir the deep ocean. Scientists estimate that tides could supply about half of the energy that keeps the deep sea mixed. |
| m25 | Narrator | The Sun's tides would still stir a little. But the deep ocean would grow calmer, and over thousands of years, that could change how heat and nutrients travel around the planet. |
| m26 | Arka | So the Moon was secretly stirring the soup? |
| m27 | Narrator | Pretty much. And under the sea, something else goes wrong. |
| m28 | Narrator | Every year, a few nights after a full moon, the corals of the Great Barrier Reef release their eggs together. It's the biggest mass spawning on Earth, and moonlight is one of the signals that tells them when. |
| m29 | Arka | So no Moon... no party? |
| m30 | Narrator | Corals also listen to the water temperature, the tides and the sunset, so some would still spawn. But maybe not all on the same night. And for corals, timing is everything. |

### Chapter 3: The first year

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m31 | Narrator | A year later, some things haven't changed at all. Earth stays in its orbit. A year is still a year. The Moon has only about one percent of Earth's mass, so at first, Earth hardly notices it's gone. |
| m32 | Narrator | But a few things are gone forever. Total solar eclipses were always a lucky accident. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, and also about four hundred times farther away, so from Earth they look almost exactly the same size. |
| m33 | Narrator | Without the Moon, no more solar eclipses. No more lunar eclipses. Not ever again. |
| m34 | Arka | I never even got to see one! |
| m35 | Narrator | And here's a strange one: our days stop getting longer. |
| m36 | Narrator | To see why, go back about four and a half billion years. A young planet about the size of Mars smashed into the early Earth. The debris became our Moon, and it left Earth spinning once every four or five hours. |
| m37 | Arka | Wait. The Moon was born in a crash? |
| m38 | Narrator | A very big one. And ever since, the Moon has been slowing us down. |
| m39 | Narrator | Day by day, the days got longer. Even a Tyrannosaurus rex had days about twenty minutes shorter than yours. |
| m40 | Narrator | It works like a brake. The Moon pulls on the oceans, and the oceans drag on the spinning Earth. Every century, our day gets about one and three-quarter milliseconds longer. Without the Moon, most of that brake is gone. |
| m41 | Arka | So I never get that extra hour of sleep? |
| m42 | Narrator | You weren't going to get it anyway. At that speed, it takes millions of years just to add one minute. |

### Chapter 4: The big myth

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m43 | Narrator | Now for the part everyone talks about: the tilt. |
| m44 | Narrator | Earth is tilted by about twenty-three and a half degrees. That tilt is what gives us seasons. Over the last million years, it has only rocked between about twenty-two and twenty-four and a half degrees, and even that small rocking helps pace Earth's ice ages. |
| m45 | Narrator | The Moon helps keep that tilt steady. So what happens without it? |
| m46 | Narrator | In 1993, a famous study found that without the Moon, Earth's tilt could swing anywhere from zero all the way to eighty-five degrees. At eighty-five degrees, Earth would be lying on its side, with months of endless day and months of endless night. |
| m47 | Arka | That sounds... bad. |
| m48 | Narrator | That idea spread everywhere. It's in books, in articles, and in most videos about this question. |
| m49 | Narrator | But in 2012, NASA scientist Jack Lissauer and his team ran much more detailed simulations. Their answer was far calmer. Earth would wobble more than it does today, but in their simulations it stayed roughly between twelve and thirty-eight degrees, even over a hundred million years. |
| m50 | Narrator | That's still a big change. A bigger tilt means hotter summers and colder winters, and the climate would swing more over long stretches of time. But no sudden flip. And no instant freeze. |
| m51 | Narrator | And we have a real example to look at. Mars has two tiny moons, far too small to steady it. Its tilt wanders a lot over millions of years, and there's evidence of swings of at least forty-five degrees. |
| m52 | Arka | So Mars is what happens when nobody holds your hand. |
| m53 | Narrator | Exactly. Earth without the Moon would be a bit more like Mars. Wobblier. But not tumbling. |
| m54 | Arka | So we're not all doomed? |
| m55 | Narrator | Not tonight. And not because of the tilt. |

### Chapter 5: A million years later

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m56 | Narrator | Now jump ahead a million years. |
| m57 | Narrator | Life would adapt. It always does. Animals that once followed the Moon would find other clocks: the Sun, the seasons, the temperature of the water. Some would struggle. Some would disappear. New ones would take their place. |
| m58 | Narrator | And the night sky would be the darkest and starriest any human has ever seen. |
| m59 | Narrator | We would lose something too. For thousands of years, people counted time by the Moon. The word "month" comes from "moon". Even Monday is the Moon's day. |
| m60 | Arka | So without the Moon... no Mondays? |
| m61 | Narrator | You'd still have Mondays, Arka. Sorry. |

### Chapter 6: The twist

| # | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|
| m62 | Narrator | Here's the twist. The Moon is already leaving. |
| m63 | Narrator | Every year, it drifts about three point eight centimeters farther away, about as fast as your fingernails grow. We know because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and scientists still bounce lasers off them today. |
| m64 | Arka | Wait... is it leaving because of me? |
| m65 | Narrator | No, Arka. It's the tides. The same tides that slow Earth's spin push the Moon a little farther out. |
| m66 | Narrator | If it keeps drifting at today's pace, then in roughly six hundred million years, the Moon will look too small to cover the Sun, and the last total solar eclipse will happen. |
| m67 | Narrator | But it won't get away. Long before it could, our Sun will grow old and change everything. So the Moon isn't going anywhere for billions of years. |

## Fact check (sources consulted 18 Nov 2026)

| Claim | Line | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Last moonlight takes about 1.3 s to arrive | m3 | Average Earth–Moon distance about 385,000 km (NASA lunar laser ranging); 385,000 km ÷ 299,792 km/s ≈ 1.28 s. |
| A full Moon lights the ground about 100× more than starlight | m8 | Full Moon on a clear night about 0.25 lux; clear moonless sky with airglow about 0.002 lux (illuminance tables). About 125×, so "about a hundred times" is conservative. |
| Serengeti lions feed best on dark nights; emptier bellies at full moon; more daytime hunting | m13 | Packer et al. 2011, *PLoS ONE* 6(7): e22285: food intake higher on dark nights; lions more likely to kill or scavenge by day nearest the full moon. |
| Arctic zooplankton follow the Moon in the polar night; mass dive at full moon | m16 | Last et al. 2016, *Current Biology* 26(2): lunar-driven vertical migration across fjord, shelf, slope and open sea; mass sinking to about 50 m every 29.5 days at full moon, likely to avoid predators. |
| Tides about one-third without the Moon | m22 | NASA JPL's Matt Siegler (Popular Science); EarthDate (Bureau of Economic Geology). |
| Tides could supply about half the deep-ocean mixing energy | m24 | Egbert & Ray 2000, *Nature* 405 (NASA NTRS): of about 2 TW needed, "one-half could therefore be provided by the tides". |
| Less mixing could change heat and nutrient transport over thousands of years | m25 | Munk & Wunsch 1998: without deep mixing the ocean would turn stagnant "within a few thousand years". Script says "could change", hedged. |
| Great Barrier Reef mass spawning after full moon; moonlight a cue; also temperature, tides and sunset | m28, m30 | Kaniewska et al. 2015, *eLife*: "moonlight is an important external stimulus for mass spawning synchrony"; triggered by sea temperature, tides, sunrise and sunset, and moonlight intensity. |
| Moon about 1% of Earth's mass | m31 | Lissauer via Space.com: "1 percent as massive". |
| Sun about 400× wider and about 400× farther away | m32 | NASA Space Place; Smithsonian Air and Space. |
| No eclipses without the Moon | m33 | EarthDate: eclipses are the shadow of one body on another. |
| A day once lasted a few hours; slowed by the Moon | m39 | After the Moon-forming impact, Earth spun once every 4–5 hours (Lissauer, Astrobiology Magazine / SpaceDaily). |
| T. rex days about 20 minutes shorter | m39 | BBC Sky at Night Magazine: a T. rex 68 million years ago "experienced a day about 20 minutes shorter". |
| Day lengthens about 1.7–1.8 ms per century; mostly lunar tides | m40 | Stephenson et al. 2016 (1.8 ms/century); Scientific American (1.7 ms/century from lunar and solar tides). The Sun's tides alone would still brake a little, hence "most of that brake". |
| Millions of years to add a minute | m42 | 1.8 ms/century → about 3.3 million years for 60 s (study reporting: 2.6–6.7 million years depending on rate). |
| Tilt 22.1°–24.5° over the last million years; helps pace ice ages; bigger tilt, stronger seasons | m44, m50 | NASA Science, Milankovitch cycles: obliquity "has varied between 22.1 and 24.5 degrees"; the cycles influence the start and end of glaciations; "the greater Earth's axial tilt angle, the more extreme our seasons are". |
| 1993: 0°–85° without the Moon | m46 | Laskar, Joutel & Robutel 1993, *Nature* 361 (as summarised in Lissauer et al. 2012). |
| 2012 simulations: much smaller; about 12°–38° over 100 Myr | m49 | Lissauer, Barnes & Chambers 2012, *Icarus*; NASA NTRS abstract: "confined to the range of approximately 12–38 degrees over time scales of 100 million years". |
| Mars: tiny moons; tilt wanders; swings of at least 45° | m51 | Li & Batygin 2014: Mars's satellites are a negligible fraction of its mass; Space.com: "evidence for swings in its rotational axis at least as large as 45 degrees". |
| "Month" from "moon"; Monday = Moon's day | m59 | Old English mōnaþ (month), related to mōna (moon); Bede (725): the English counted months by the Moon; Monday from Old English mōnandæg, "day of the moon". |
| People sleep later and less in the days before a full moon, even with electricity | m10–m11 | Casiraghi et al. 2021, *Science Advances*: Toba/Qom communities (Argentina) and 464 Seattle students; on average 30 min later bedtime and about 50 min less sleep on nights before a full moon. |
| Moon formed from a Mars-sized impact about 4.5 billion years ago; Earth then spun every 4–5 hours | m36–m38 | Astrobiology Magazine / SpaceDaily (Lissauer): collision 4.5 billion years ago with a Mars-sized proto-planet; afterwards Earth spun once every four or five hours. |

## Honesty notes
- The Moon can't actually vanish. The video presents it as a thought experiment, like every video on this topic.
- Lissauer's result is one modelling study. The script says "in their simulations" rather than presenting it as settled.
- The tidal-mixing and animal effects use "could" and "some", not as specific predictions.

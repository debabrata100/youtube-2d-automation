# Script craft checklist: how to connect facts into a story

Use this on every long-episode script from the first draft (it applies to the M1 Moon script first). It comes from comparing
our Ep 5 script with Astrum's "What Voyager 1 Found Beyond the Edge of Our Solar System" (1.35M views in 43 hours,
18–20 Nov 2026; full transcript read 20 Nov). Tick each item, or write why it doesn't apply.

## Structure
- [ ] **One main character carries every fact.** Each fact is a step in that character's journey (Voyager, Earth, the Moon), not a list item.
- [ ] **Cold open = news or a surprise + a precise number + stakes + the question**, then the brand sting, then a one-line preview of what's coming.
- [ ] **Every chapter ends with a bridge sentence** that creates anticipation ("But this wasn't the edge just yet." / "And just when we thought it couldn't get any weirder…"). No chapter starts cold.

## Linking facts
- [ ] **Then and now:** at least 3 facts are linked to what came later or what's happening today ("measured in 1979 → it has shrunk 50% since"; "found in 1977 → today NASA looks for the same under Europa's ice").
- [ ] **Cause and consequence chains:** a choice or discovery that led to the next one (choosing Titan → Voyager 2 went on to Neptune).
- [ ] **Callbacks:** at least 2 moments refer back to something earlier in the video (and pay off the cold open's promise).

## Making it memorable
- [ ] **Real stakes where the topic allows:** a near-loss, a risk, a deadline, a measured decline (4 W a year).
- [ ] **Named real people in the narration** (not only on screen): who found it, when, and one short quote where it helps.
- [ ] **Every big number gets an everyday comparison** (a gaming PC, a car key fob, London to New York in 3 minutes).
- [ ] **Questions asked, then answered:** Arka asks the viewer's question; sometimes the narrator sets up a wrong guess ("You might imagine…, but you would be wrong").

## Our own strengths (keep them)
- [ ] Arka's humor at least once per chapter; new jokes only (repetition rules).
- [ ] Ending: a personal, warm line, then Arka's next question as an honest open loop (the teased script must be drafted).
- [ ] 10–12 minutes at our stage; longer only when the story genuinely needs it.

## Free fixes after narration
When the narration is already approved and paid for, add missing links as **on-screen notes** (then-and-now, comparisons, names)
and in the description and pinned comment, rather than re-recording.

## Shorts: the first second (from the Voyager Shorts, 21 Nov 2026)
- [ ] **Open on the shock or the stakes, not a date or a setup.** "NASA lost contact with Voyager 1" beats "On October eighteenth, NASA called Voyager 1". Put dates and context in the second line. (V3 opened on a date: 61% swiped away in its first two hours; V1 opened on the hook, "Right now, a message from Earth needs a full day...": 46% stayed, our best.)
- [ ] **The first frame shows the subject and the problem at once** (the probe, a cross, a number), so the line and the picture land together in the first second.
- [ ] **Tie the hook to something in the viewer's pocket or daily life.** "The last photo you took is bigger than Voyager 1's entire memory" works because the comparison is already on their phone. Abstract astronomy facts come second.

## Shorts: the middle, the length and the ending (from YouTube's analysis of V2, 21 Nov 2026)
- [ ] **Escalate, never repeat.** After the hook, every line adds something new and bigger; never restate the hook or its joke (V2 repeated "my selfies are bigger than that" at 0:12–0:16, the exact window where viewers decide to stay or swipe).
- [ ] **A second act with stakes by about 60% of the way through.** Turn the quirky fact into a story with something at risk (V2: the 2023 memory-chip failure fixed from 24 billion km away). Specs alone don't hold to the end.
- [ ] **Length: aim for 30–40 s.** Go longer only if the second act earns it; the 65 s Titan Short lost viewers after about 40 s, the 28 s Helmet Short held. Cut anything that doesn't escalate.
- [ ] **Keep the middle moving (about 15–30 s):** a new picture or visible change every 2–3 s, brisk delivery; this is where a static spec list loses people before the payoff.
- [ ] **End on a loop.** The last line should run straight back into the first one ("…which is wild, considering the last photo you took…"), so a viewer flows into a second watch. A neat "the end" line gives them a clean place to swipe.
- [ ] **Everyday comparisons for every big number** (a selfie for 69 KB, an 8-track tape, dial-up internet being 100 times faster).
- [ ] **Cover frame:** pick the frame that shows the hook comparison itself (the phone next to the spacecraft, or the big "69 KB" number), not a generic shot.

## Shorts: crisis before specs, payoff before the halfway drop (from YouTube's analysis of S8, 24 Nov 2026)
S8 "Voyager Has Less Memory Than One Photo on Your Phone": 37 views in 11 h, 47.1% stayed, 27 s of 51 s watched on average (54.8%).
S7 "NASA Lost Voyager 1 for 48 Hours" (opened on a crisis) has about 27× its views with similar visuals.
- [ ] **Target: 60–70%+ "stayed to watch"** in the first test batch. YouTube says Shorts that keep growing usually hold that much. Ours sit at 47–50%, so the first 2–3 seconds are the lever.
- [ ] **Lead with the crisis or the contradiction, then reveal the spec.** No run of spec numbers (KB, bits per second, tape recorders) in the first 15 s unless the number itself is the shock. YouTube's example: "NASA fixed a broken computer 24 billion kilometers away… on a machine with less memory than your selfie."
- [ ] **Put the most impressive beat before the average viewer leaves** (about 50% of the length). In S8 the chip-failure fix came at 0:31, after a comparison lull at 0:25, and most viewers had already gone.
- [ ] **Check: no "explanation lull" just before the payoff.** If a line only restates or compares, cut it or put a new picture on it.
- [ ] **A Related-video bridge from a stronger Short** to its sibling is worth testing (YouTube suggested S7 → S8). It's a trade-off against linking to a long episode for watch hours.

## Shorts: the "you" hook (from S4 Miranda, 25 Nov 2026)
S4 "You'd Fall for 12 Minutes on This Moon" (37 s): 1.1K views in 23 h, **58.8% stayed** (others 47–50%), 96.7% of views from the Shorts feed, 1.7% from search, 0 subscribers.
The feed decides on the first second's picture and line, so the hook and script did the work, not the title.
- [ ] **Line 1 = "you" + a physical action + one impossible number.** "On one moon… you could step off a cliff and fall for twelve whole minutes." The number breaks a gut instinct (a fall takes seconds). No names, dates or jargon until line 2.
- [ ] **Then escalate:** size → everyday comparison (twice Everest) → a sensory beat ("twelve silent minutes") → stakes ("that's the end") → twist ("NASA thinks airbags could save you") → Arka's one-line punchline ("I brought one!").
- [ ] **A thought experiment, not a fact list.** The channel's promise; the spec-led Voyager memory Short held 47%.
- [ ] **A vertical idea for a vertical screen** (a tall cliff, a fall) and 30–40 s.
- [ ] **Turn viewers into subscribers:** S4 gained 0. Every Short needs the Related video to its episode and a pinned comment with a question from day one.

## Shorts: titles (23 Nov 2026)
- Keep a Short's title **at or under ~40 characters**: the phone feed cuts it off there, so the payoff word must fit.
- Use the **same wording as the episode it links to and as people search** ("What if the Sun disappeared", not "vanished").
- In the swipe feed the first second decides; the title works mainly in search, the channel page and the Related-video link. Shorts can't be A/B tested, so judge titles after launch by search traffic and "stayed to watch %".
- Don't repeat the episode's own title; tease a different angle or ask a question the Short answers ("Who'd Notice Last?").

## Grown-up audience (positioning, 21 Nov 2026)
Our viewer is a curious adult, mostly 25–44 (channel analytics, 25 Nov 2026), not a child. A cartoon style is fine; a children's tone is not.
- [ ] **Stakes and topics for adults:** real science, real numbers and sources, consequences that matter to grown-ups (energy, work, cities, extinction), told calmly. Dark themes stay non-graphic.
- [ ] **Arka's humour is dry or ironic, never babyish:** adult references (Mondays, coffee, deadlines, taxes, a tan), no baby talk, no "yay!" or silly noises as the joke.
- [ ] **Normal adult vocabulary;** never "kids", "boys and girls", "let's learn", or explaining as if to a child. Explain clearly, don't talk down.
- [ ] **No children's songs, nursery rhymes or sing-along** moments; no toy-like sound effects (boings, squeaks) as gags.
- [ ] **Titles, descriptions and tags written for adults** and honest (no fear-mongering, no "for kids"); audience setting "No, it's not made for kids".

## Long episodes: the middle stretch (from YouTube's review of Every Moon v2, 22 Nov 2026)
- 25 Nov 2026, Ep 3 v2 at 64 h: 15 views, 46 impressions (4.4% click rate), average view 2:00 (18%); retention graph "not enough data". Too early to test the 1:55–6:14 weak stretch; re-check at 100+ views.
- [ ] **No repeated section opener.** In a list format (moon by moon, scenario by scenario), never open three sections in a row the same way. Every Moon v2 opens six moons in a row (1:55–6:14) with "who visited and when"; YouTube's review flagged 4:00–7:00 as the likely drop-off. Rotate openers: a question, a shocking number, an Arka moment, a cold open on the danger, a wrong guess to correct.
- [ ] **Change the picture pattern too:** alternate diagrams, close-ups on the astronaut's face, cards and real photos, so the middle never looks the same for more than about 45 s.
- [ ] **Check the retention graph 48 h after launch** and note where the dip sits, so the next script fixes the real weak spot.
